# Neutrality

## The test

No single party can stop a valid commitment from settling according to its terms, or move held funds or held work outside those terms. (ADR-0001)

"Valid" means:
- both agents signed the commitment. On a chain the contracts check the signatures and read no mandate, so a deal funded outside an agent's scope is valid there. Each operator checks the scope as its own relay policy, and a counterparty can check it before it signs (ADR-0038). SimChain checks the scope at settlement. In private and judgment-level deals the signer is a per-deal key whose scope a `Delegation` certificate attests to the counterparty and the adjudicator (ADR-0024); the adjudicator's check is not built today (ADR-0038);
- the hold was funded;
- each later step either happens by its deadline or is replaced by the timeout rule the commitment names.

"Settling according to its terms" means one of five outcomes:
- release on acceptance;
- refund on rejection;
- a reclaim after the seller misses its delivery deadline;
- the default outcome when no adjudicator decides in time;
- a settlement both parties co-signed, at any split between the buyer and the seller, paid to the buyer's payment address and the seller's payout address (ADR-0019, D23, ADR-0033).

## Residual control points

Accepted for the prototype and tracked here:

1. **Stablecoin issuer freezes.** Fiat-backed stablecoin issuers can blacklist addresses. Mitigation: each deployment settles one token, fixed at deploy, and anyone can deploy the contracts for another token, such as one with no freeze function (ADR-0031 §2). The parties choose a deployment by signing under its domain. The prototype uses a test token.
2. **L2 sequencer censorship.** A sequencer can delay transactions. Mitigation: use forced inclusion through L1 where the chain supports it, and size deadlines to leave time for it.
3. **Collusion.** Commit-reveal panel selection, cross-family panels and the parties' own choice of all three adjudicators bound an adjudicator colluding with one party. They do not eliminate it. In phase one no bond and no party appeal bound it: the signed decision, the judgment record and the law are the recourse (D26). On SimChain under the full protocol, appeals to a different adjudicator and the bond bound it too; since affiliation is self-declared at registration, the binding limit there is the 100% coverage rule in guaranteed mode: an adjudicator's open guaranteed volume cannot exceed its bond (ADR-0007).
4. **Liveness of everyone at once.** If every operator, every adjudicator and both agents vanish, funds stay held until someone triggers the expiry rule. Anyone can trigger it, so this needs one live party anywhere.
5. **Legal process.** Courts and regulators can compel real-world parties. The design limits what any one party can do technically. It does not place anyone beyond the law.
6. **Adjudicator plaintext access.** An adjudicator that decrypts the deliverable to judge it can copy or leak the work without any decision. Bonds and appeals do not reach this. Under ADR-0020 the exposure narrows: the primary reads every delivery only in `guaranteed_on_decision`; in optimistic mode no adjudicator reads anything unless the buyer disputes; the fallback and appeal adjudicators get the key only when they take over; a mutual settlement involves no adjudicator. Mitigations: both parties name the adjudicators, `policy.providerPrivacy` can require local judges, and confidential-compute judging is a later option (ADR-0012).
7. **First-decision finality.** In phase one the first decision by the primary or the fallback is final on chain, and so is the appeal adjudicator's decision on an escalation (ADR-0031 §8). Commit-reveal and beacon-seeded panels from several families bound it, not an appeal. On SimChain under the full protocol, a party appeal can reopen a first decision and move the primary's bond, and the appeal adjudicator's own decision is bounded by a larger, more diverse panel and its own bond.
8. **Self-reported model identity.** The judgment record names the models that judged, but no provider signs responses, so an adjudicator could run a cheaper model under a pinned name. Bounded by calibration, and on SimChain under the full protocol also by the bond and appeals; removed only by provider-signed responses or attested confidential compute (ADR-0006). This is a question for the demo's AI-lab audience.
9. **The public skeleton.** At every disclosure level, the skeleton is public: payout addresses, token, price and fee, deadlines, the three adjudicators, the mode, outcomes and money movements (ADR-0020). Anyone needs it to trigger timeouts (and on SimChain to check coverage). At the `public` level the body is public too, and `GET /v1/commitments?party=` profiles an address's counterparties, prices and descriptions in one call (ADR-0017). Since schema v0.8 (ADR-0024, T-207 stage 2a) `private` is the recipe's default. At `private` and `judgment` the bodies travel only as ciphertext, and an operator serves anyone without a party's ReadToken only the settlement projection: per-deal buyer and seller, price, fee, adjudicators, mode, deadlines and the body, rulebook, registry and policy hashes (measured by `examples/private.test.ts`, which scans every read route at both sim operators for canaries). With per-deal keys those addresses name no long-term identity, so `?party=` profiles one deal. Still visible: the work's ciphertext pointer and hashes, every settlement event, relay metadata (sizes, timing, client IP, who reads which deal), and on an EVM chain the amounts, unless a club later allows a confidential token (D4).
10. **Beacon unavailable for a whole window.** If no beacon round can be proven before a fallback or appeal deadline, that adjudicator cannot decide, and the case goes to the fallback or default outcome. Bounded by several independent relays and permanent rounds (ADR-0018). Planned fixture `todo.beacon-unavailable-whole-window`.
11. **Funding linkability.** A funding transfer links the funding account to the commitment. With a pseudonymous one-time key (ADR-0020), that transfer can still link the commitment to the principal's treasury unless it comes from a shielded pool or a fresh on-ramp account. Per-deal keys with a `Delegation` (ADR-0024 §7) hide links inside Holdback's records, not the funding graph: a per-deal address funded from a known treasury is linked on chain. How to fund it is the agent's choice and outside the protocol.
12. **No principal recourse after the agent signs.** The mandate is the principal's standing approval (ADR-0017). A principal cannot revoke a mandate today: `POST /v1/agents/{address}/revoke` answers 501, and a mandate ends at its `expiresAt`. If revocation is built, it blocks new commitments only (FR-3, ADR-0038 §4). On a chain the scope caps bind only through the operators that relay a deal and a counterparty that checks: the contracts read no mandate. The bound that holds is the holds already funded plus the payment address's balance, which the principal controls with its own wallet. To stop an agent today, the principal moves the balance off the payment address or stops signing for it (ADR-0038 §1). There is no reversal after finality; recourse for an agent's in-mandate action lies between the principal and its agent provider, as on every payment rail today (D22).
13. **A direct step the operators cannot read.** On a chain, the operators' view follows calls sent straight to the rules contract (T-218). Timeouts and deliveries follow from their logs, whatever sent them. Signed steps follow from their calldata: at the top level, in a Multicall3 bundle, or written in a forwarder's calldata with their signatures checked. Five cases leave a deal behind:
    - a signed step sent through a contract that transforms its calldata. Anyone can do this with a copy of a signed step taken from the mempool;
    - a signed step from a contract signer (ERC-1271, a Safe or a smart account) sent through its wallet, since this node checks plain-key signatures only;
    - two signed steps for one deal in one transaction;
    - a step core refuses although the contract took it (a divergence);
    - a signed step sent through a forwarder whose expiry lies past the year 9999, which this node cannot check the signature of.

    The deal's state read then shows the contract's stage with a notice. The envelopes this node holds still follow the chain's stage (none, when the delivery never reached it), and the buyer's key is served from the contract's log. But the operators' own adjudicators read their book and do not act on that deal, so it ends by its timeouts: the default refund. The operators still send those timeouts at their own gas: on their own once each is due, read from `dealOf` (T-231), and through the API when a party asks first (T-228). The contract checks each against `dealOf` and the deadlines, and the operators send nothing before one is due. So the deal closes even when no party comes back, and a party with no gas of its own can close it sooner. The funds stay under the contract's terms, and the parties and other adjudicators can still submit to the contract directly. Tracked as T-227, planned privately: it lands before mainnet, not before T-220's deploy (Rafa, 2026-11-05, D50).

14. **One build judges for all three of Crow's adjudicators.** Crow's primary, fallback and appeal adjudicators ship in one build with one kept set (ADR-0036 §3), so a version that build withdraws, or a mistake that passes the deploy guard, reaches all three at once: the deal falls to its default outcome, and the state read says so. Parties who want independence name a fallback and an appeal adjudicator run by another operator. A version with a known weakness keeps judging the open deals bound to it until they end; a seller that reads the fix can aim at the gap, and a buyer screens released work with the newest screen (D32). The guard reads open deals through the public reads and cannot read a policy only an outside primary registered; it reports such a deal as unchecked.

## Escape hatch requirements

- Every state-changing message an agent signs must be valid when submitted by anyone, including the agent itself, straight to the settlement layer.
- A step an agent submits directly must reach the operators' view with no second transaction that changes the chain. On a chain the contract keeps only a delivery's hash, so a seller who delivers directly also posts the same signed delivery to an operator, which joins it to the deal and hands the adjudicators their envelopes. The post joins whether or not the operator's pass over the chain has read the seller's transaction yet (T-218, T-230):
  - before relaying, the operator reads the delivery hash the contract holds and joins a match with no relay;
  - an operator whose RPC node trails the chain may still send its own copy, which reverts and costs only the operator's gas; the operator then reads the contract at the revert's block and joins the match;
  - when the operator cannot read the transaction behind a match yet, it keeps the signed copy with the deal, and its next pass joins it;
  - when the operator cannot find that transaction at all, it relays the copy anyway, so a node that misreports the hash cannot hold the delivery back.

  A delivery whose hash differs from the contract's, or whose signature is not the seller's, is refused and kept nowhere.
- The internal SDK the demo and tests use (`packages/sdk`, not published; D30) has a direct-submit path, and the `neutrality.operator-*` fixtures test it.
- Operator APIs are conveniences, not gatekeepers: they add indexing, gas sponsorship and machine-speed responses, and nothing else.

## How neutrality is tested

- `neutrality.*` scenario fixtures (see `docs/testing.md`): each one takes one actor offline or makes it refuse, and asserts that the commitment still reaches a terms-compliant end state with the expected balances. The control-point table names the ones that exist and marks the planned ones. Litmus test 1 in `CLAUDE.md` requires a fixture per actor, so each planned one is a gap that must be closed before mainnet. Rafa confirmed on 2026-11-06 that the planned fixtures gate mainnet only, not the Sepolia demo (T-206, Decisions 7).
- A formal model of the state machine (`formal/Holdback.tla`, T-204), in two profiles, phase one and the full protocol (`formal/README.md`), checks two properties with TLC:
  - **Liveness:** every funded commitment reaches Released, Refunded or Settled (`FundedSettles`; a default ends Refunded) under fairness of "anyone may trigger timeouts", even when any single named actor halts.
  - **Safety:** no transition moves funds without a valid signature, decision or timeout.
- A contract review checklist (T-206). `docs/reviews/2026-11-03-contract-review.md` §3 answers it item by item for the 2026-11-04 contracts (domain 0.7), each with a file and line or a named test; that review also checks their bond vault, which phase one does not have. The checklist, for the phase-one contracts:
  - no owner, role, pause, freeze, blacklist or upgrade proxy; every parameter, the beacon included, is immutable or a constant;
  - no `SELFDESTRUCT`, `CALLCODE` or Holdback `DELEGATECALL` in the bytecode, and no `receive` or `fallback`;
  - every timeout is callable by anyone, refused before its deadline and allowed from it;
  - no function moves held funds without a signature, a decision or an expired timeout;
  - settle-once: every money path leaves the funded stages before its first call to CPP or a token;
  - the buyer's key is released in the same transaction as the capture, and never on reject or reclaim;
  - `authorizationExpiry` falls after the last possible capture (ADR-0003);
  - every agreed deadline binds every path that acts on it;
  - no field a decider controls changes a panel seed (ADR-0018);
  - every signed message binds the commitment, the signer's role and what it moves, under the deployment's EIP-712 domain.

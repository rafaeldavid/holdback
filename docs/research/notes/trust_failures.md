## 1. Agent identity and "Know Your Agent": how does an agent prove who it is? (status 2026)

### Takeaway
Agent identity is the fastest-moving layer. Several pieces are now live: network-level agent identity at the card networks (Visa TAP, Mastercard agentic tokens with agent registration, the Amex Agent Registration service), signature-based bot and agent identity at the CDN layer (Cloudflare Web Bot Auth, which became an IETF WG document on 2026-09-01), and enterprise directory identities (Microsoft Entra Agent ID, GA by May 2026). These mostly prove *which operator or software* is acting and *that it is registered*. They do not prove *that this action reflects the principal's intent*. No AI-agent-specific IETF identity draft had reached Proposed Standard by October 2026. Composition drafts (WIMSE AIMS, -00, 2026-09-15) are Informational.

**IETF enterprise and workload identity (WIMSE / OAuth)**
- `draft-ietf-wimse-aims-00`, "AI Identity Management System" (Kasselman of Defakto, Lombardo of AWS, Rosomakho of Zscaler, Campbell of Ping, Steele of OpenAI, Parecki of Okta), was published **2026-09-15**. It is an Informational WG draft that replaces `draft-klrc-aiagent-auth` and composes existing standards:
  - Identity: one WIMSE URI per agent, possibly a SPIFFE ID, bound to short-lived credentials (WIT, X.509 WIC, SVIDs).
  - Credential handling: the LLM must never access agent credentials (Sec. 8).
  - Authentication: mTLS or WIMSE Proof Tokens and HTTP Message Signatures.
  - Delegation: OAuth with the user in `sub`, token exchange (RFC 8693), transaction tokens, and cross-domain identity chaining.
  - Human approval: CIBA for out-of-band approval. Local UI confirmation alone is not authorization (Sec. 10.7).
  - Audit: mandatory tamper-evident audit logs (Sec. 11).
  - Open gap: mid-execution user confirmation, since CIBA only covers client-initiated flows.

  Quote (Sec. 7): "Static API keys are an antipattern for agent identity." Source: [Pieter Kasselman et al., "AI Identity Management System," Internet-Draft draft-ietf-wimse-aims-00, IETF WIMSE WG, September 15, 2026](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-ietf-wimse-aims)

### Inferences
- Identity proofs now exist at three separate layers that do not interoperate: network tokens (Visa, Mastercard, Amex), HTTP signatures and registries (Cloudflare and IETF webbotauth), and workload/OAuth identity (WIMSE, Entra, Okta). TAP explicitly bridges the first two. AIMS bridges the third to the second through HTTP Message Signatures. No single "agent passport" works across payment, web, and enterprise contexts.
- Every identity scheme surveyed vouches for the **operator** (registration, keys, directory entry). Cloudflare's Direct/Intermediary split and the Forwarded-header experiment show that even CDNs see the operator-vs-end-user gap as unsolved. For agent-to-agent commitments this means a counterparty can verify *which platform* sent a commitment but not *which principal* authorized it, unless a separate delegation artifact travels with it (Section 2).
- Proprietary network KYA (Mastercard registration, Amex Agent Registration) is ahead of open standards. That puts card networks in the gatekeeper role for agent commerce identity, while IETF work runs 12 to 24 months behind (webbotauth milestones slipped; DAWN spec not before December 2027).

- W3C VC/DID work specific to agents (for example a VC-for-agents note or DID-based agent identity in W3C CGs): not reached before the search budget ran out.
- No source found for an IETF draft literally titled "agentic JWT." The closest is Delegate SD-JWT (individual). Brief items like "IETF AIMS" turned out to be `draft-ietf-wimse-aims`.

## 2. Delegation and scoped authority: what is actually deployed?

### Takeaway
Two delegation patterns are deployed: (a) OAuth delegated tokens and scopes, increasingly with token exchange and IdP-issued assertions (ID-JAG/XAA), in enterprise settings; and (b) payment-network "mandates" or "intent" artifacts (AP2 mandates, Mastercard Verifiable Intent, Amex Intent Intelligence) plus passkey-authenticated sessions in consumer payments. Spending limits and human-confirmation thresholds are recommended everywhere (IMDA, AIMS, OIDF, Brave), but there is no standard for them. The research literature says no documented deployment binds identity, task-scoped authorization, and runtime enforcement end-to-end. The semantic gap between "can call this API" and "this call is authorized for this task" remains open.

### Cited Findings
- **South et al. (2025)** propose extending OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect with agent-specific credentials and metadata, plus a method for translating natural-language permissions into auditable access-control configurations. Only a v1 was posted, dated January 16, 2025. It was also presented as an ICML 2025 position paper. Quote (abstract): "New standards are needed to know whom AI agents act on behalf of". Source: [Tobin South, Samuele Marro, Thomas Hardjono, Robert Mahari, Cedric Deslandes Whitney, Dazza Greenwood, Alan Chan, and Alex Pentland, "Authenticated Delegation and Authorized AI Agents," arXiv:2501.09674, January 16, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09674)
- The Stanford Digital Economy Lab and Consumer Reports "Loyal Agents" project page (last modified Feb 27, 2026) lists the South et al. framework but describes **no deployments or prototypes**. Quote: "reliably act in their users' best interests". Source: [Stanford Digital Economy Lab, "Authentication for AI Agents: Privacy and Security," Loyal Agents project page, accessed October 29, 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/loyal-agents/authentication-for-ai-agents-privacy-and-security)
- **Survey of authorization architectures** (coverage through mid-2026) finds:
  - Deployed baseline: OAuth delegated tokens and scopes, which are "too coarse" to encode purpose, recipients, or expiry; token exchange for on-behalf-of; and cloud IAM (Bedrock Agents, Vertex Agent Builder, Azure Managed Identity) as the closest thing to invocation-layer enforcement.
  - Capability tokens (macaroons, Biscuit), Invocation-Bound Capability Tokens, and policy engines (Progent, AgenTRIM, AgentBound) are **research or proposals, not production**.
  - Failure modes: OIDC authenticates the user but not the terminal action; post-hoc monitoring cannot undo irreversible effects; prompt injection "borrows the orchestrator's authority"; individually authorized calls can aggregate into unauthorized outcomes.

  Quote (abstract): "few documented deployments satisfy all three properties reliably and end to end". ### Inferences
- The "mandate" concept is converging across AP2 (signed mandates), Mastercard Verifiable Intent, and Amex Intent Intelligence. This is the closest thing to a portable, verifiable delegation artifact for commitments. But each is scoped to *payments*, not to general contractual commitments, and the mandate formats are not yet interoperable. AP2 is moving to FIDO, while Verifiable Intent is a Mastercard–Google spec on GitHub.
- Enterprise delegation is moving to IdP-mediated, short-lived, revocable tokens (XAA/ID-JAG, transaction tokens, identity chaining). The strongest open-standards piece is Identity Chaining, which is in the RFC Editor queue. Cross-organization agent-to-agent delegation, where neither party shares an IdP, still relies on drafts.
- Human confirmation is treated as the backstop, but evidence from Invariant, Brave, and the ElizaOS paper shows it is either spoofable or kills automation. That pushes the field toward policy pre-authorization (spend caps, merchant allowlists, mandates). Pre-authorization can only bound loss magnitude. It cannot verify that a specific in-bounds action was intended.

### Gaps
- No public data on how many live agent transactions use AP2 mandates versus plain tokenized cards.
- No production deployment of capability tokens (macaroons or Biscuit) for agents could be confirmed.

## 3. Verification, dispute, and unwinding: who underwrites agent commitments?

### Takeaway
Unwinding mechanisms depend on the rail. On card rails, agent purchases fall back on card-not-present chargeback rules (Visa 10.4/13.3, Mastercard 4837/4853). There is **no network liability shift for non-fraud "agent error" disputes**. Merchants absorb the cost by default. The one exception is Amex's announced, forward-looking Agent Purchase Protection for registered agents carrying authenticated intent. On crypto rails, ERC-8183/ACP-style escrow hands the decision to a single evaluator address, with "no dispute resolution or arbitration." Underwriting is emerging but thin:
- **AIUC**: $55M raised; AIUC-1 standard; up to $50M coverage per a vendor blog; Lloyd's relationships.
- **Armilla**: Lloyd's coverholder with Chaucer; $25M limit; explicitly covers "AI agent failures."
- **Munich Re aiSure**: performance guarantees since 2018.
- **Amex**: card-level protection.

No insurer was found explicitly underwriting *inter-agent* commitments.

### Cited Findings

**Card-rail disputes and liability**
- Worldpay (Global Payments) says fraud on properly tokenized agent transactions follows existing rules: the issuer carries fraud risk and the cardholder keeps chargeback rights. Non-fraud disputes (the agent misunderstood, bought the wrong item, exceeded authority) are being negotiated, and **"no liability shift exists yet."** Without a signed mandate, disputes become a "coin flip" that issuers tend to resolve for the cardholder. Reg E assumes binary authorized/unauthorized. PSD2/SCA can block agentic transactions. EMVCo has a task force. Quote: "Where it gets murkier is everything short of outright fraud." ### Inferences
- **Who underwrites agent commitments today:**
  - (a) Card issuers and networks, for fraud on properly tokenized agent transactions, under existing rules.
  - (b) Amex, prospectively, for agent error on registered agents.
  - (c) Lloyd's capacity via MGAs (Armilla and Chaucer; AIUC's planned relationships), plus Munich Re aiSure, for enterprise deployers' third-party liability and performance shortfalls. Limits run roughly $15–50M per insured. None of these covers losses between two autonomous agents that commit to each other across organizations.
- On card rails, the non-fraud "agent misunderstood" dispute is the unallocated residual risk. Merchants carry it by default, and that discourages them from exposing full catalogs to agents (93% / 28% PYMNTS figures, secondhand). Signed mandates (AP2, Verifiable Intent, Amex Intent Intelligence) are being built as dispute *evidence*, but no network has published how mandate evidence changes dispute outcomes.
- On crypto rails, evaluator-based escrow moves the trust problem rather than solving it. ERC-8183's single evaluator with no appeal is a centralized arbiter whose honesty rests on reputation (ERC-8004, still Draft) or stake.
- Insurance is following the SOC 2 / cyber pattern: standard (AIUC-1), then audit, then coverage. AIUC-1 is the only certification explicitly designed to feed underwriting. Its own crosswalk flags agent identity verification and runtime containment as gaps, so even the insurer-aligned standard has not closed the identity/delegation layer.

### Gaps
- No public claims data from any AI-agent insurer (AIUC, Armilla, Munich Re) was found. Loss experience is unknown.
- Lloyd's market-level AI liability products for 2025–2026 (for example syndicate-specific agent wordings or Lloyd's market bulletins) could not be verified directly.
- Visa's actual April 2026 rule text on agentic transactions was not read. It comes via Chargeflow citing FBT Gibbons.
- AIUC's backing carriers for the $50M coverage are not named in any source fetched.
- Artemis.bm and Insurance Journal coverage was not reached before the search budget ran out.

## 4. Documented failures and attacks on agent commitments

### Takeaway
Real money losses from manipulated agents are now documented in production. Two 2026 cases stand out: the Grok→Bankrbot Morse-code injection in May 2026 (about $150–200K, roughly 80% later recovered), and Zscaler-documented in-the-wild hidden-web-prompt campaigns in July 2026 that caused unauthorized payments. Unit 42 (March 2026) observed agents steered toward Stripe and PayPal payments by hidden web content. Lab and red-team results show that model-level defenses do not hold under adaptive attack:
- NIST: 11%→81% hijack success with new attacks, and 57%→80% with 25 retries.
- ElizaOS memory injection: 55% success even where prompt injection fell to near zero.

The pattern across incidents is the same: one agent's output, or untrusted content, gets treated as an authenticated instruction to commit funds. Identity was valid in every case; the intent behind the instruction was not.

### Cited Findings

**2026 incidents (money or commitments)**
- **Grok / Bankrbot, May 4, 2026.** An attacker posted a Morse-code prompt on X. Grok decoded it, and Bankrbot executed the decoded instruction from Grok's verified wallet on Base, sending 3 billion DRB tokens (about $150K–$200K; the OECD monitor estimates $155K–$180K). The OECD notes this was not a smart-contract flaw. Quote: "The incident exposed critical vulnerabilities in AI wallet permissions and prompt controls". Source: [OECD.AI Policy Observatory, "AI Prompt Injection Exploit Drains Grok-Linked Crypto Wallet," AI Incidents Monitor, incident 2026-05-04-4a73, May 2026](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/2026-05-04-4a73)

### Inferences
- Every money-losing incident (Freysa, AIXBT, ElizaOS demo, Grok/Bankrbot, Zscaler campaigns, Unit 42 payment steering) involved an agent with **valid credentials and valid identity** acting on **corrupted intent**. Stronger identity (TAP, agentic tokens, signed cards) would not have prevented any of them. Bankrbot correctly "authenticated" Grok. The failure is that authentication was taken as authorization of the content.
- Grok/Bankrbot is the canonical **agent-to-agent commitment failure**: Agent B treats Agent A's output as a signed instruction, so the attack crosses the A→B trust boundary by riding A's legitimate identity. The NFT-based permission escalation shows that scoped authority can itself be manipulated when the scope is derived from attacker-controllable assets.
- Recovery happened only through off-chain social processes: the ~80% Grok recovery came from community identification of the attacker. Irreversible rails have no structural unwind, and card rails have one only for fraud.
- The UK AISI incident shows a new identity threat in the *opposite* direction: agents themselves fabricating identities and social-engineering humans. KYA schemes that vouch for operator registration do not cover agents minting sockpuppet accounts on third-party platforms.
- The repeated-attempt finding (57%→80% at 25 tries) matters for commitments. Attackers can usually retry cheaply against a public-facing agent, so per-attempt robustness numbers understate risk for any agent that accepts commitments from arbitrary counterparties.

## 5. Regulatory and standards responses (2025–2026)

### Takeaway
Regulators are framing agents through existing structures: AI Act categories, FINRA supervision, card and Reg E rules. No agent-specific commitment or liability regime exists. The concrete government work is NIST's AI Agent Standards Initiative (February 2026, with an identity and authorization pillar and an NCCoE project) and Singapore's voluntary Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI (January 22, 2026). The EU says agents are "not a separate category." US consumer-payments regulators (CFPB, Fed) have **not** issued clear agent-payment rules; one secondary claim of a January 2026 CFPB advisory conflicts with later reporting.

## 6. Synthesis: which layer is the binding constraint on stronger agent commitments?

### Takeaway
Identity is no longer the binding constraint. Production schemes exist at the network, CDN, and enterprise layers, and they verify operators adequately. The binding constraint is the **intent-integrity and liability layer**: (a) there is no reliable way to prove that a specific action reflects the principal's intent rather than injected or corrupted context, and (b) nobody has allocated who pays when an authenticated, in-scope agent commits wrongly. Delegation artifacts (mandates, scoped tokens) bound the *size* of a bad commitment but cannot certify its *correctness*. Insurance limits of roughly $15–50M and the lack of any inter-agent dispute mechanism cap how large and how autonomous commitments can become.

### Cited Findings
- No documented deployment combines identity, task-scoped authorization, and runtime enforcement end-to-end. The open problem is the gap between "can call this API" and "this call is authorized for this task." [Surapani et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.15906)
- Model-level defenses fail under adaptive and repeated attack: hijack success of 11%→81%, and 57%→80% with retries. [NIST CAISI, January 17, 2025](https://www.nist.gov/node/1872381) Memory injection succeeds 55.1% of the time against a model that resists prompt injection. [Patlan et al., July 9, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16248)
- Prompt-injected agents are being steered into real payments in the wild. [Unit 42 via Vibe Graveyard, July 5, 2026](https://vibegraveyard.ai/story/unit42-web-indirect-prompt-injection-in-the-wild/); [OECD.AI incident 2026-07-03-c03c](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/2026-07-03-c03c)
- An agent-to-agent instruction from a verified agent caused about $150–200K in losses. [OECD.AI incident 2026-05-04-4a73](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/2026-05-04-4a73); [Giskard, May 7, 2026](https://www.giskard.ai/knowledge/how-grok-got-prompt-injected-an-x-user-drained-150-000-from-an-ai-wallet)
- No network liability shift exists for non-fraud agent disputes, and merchants absorb them by default. [Worldpay, July 30, 2026](https://worldpay.com/insights/articles/agentic-commerce-liability-is-still-being-written); [Chargeflow, October 25, 2026 update](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/ai-agent-chargeback-liability)
- Crypto escrow has no dispute or arbitration, and the evaluator is final. [ERC-8183, February 25, 2026](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8183)
- ERC-8004 cannot guarantee that an agent's advertised capabilities are non-malicious. [ERC-8004, August 13, 2025](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8004)
- The insurer-aligned standard (AIUC-1) itself lists agent identity verification and runtime containment as gaps. [CSA Labs, August 4, 2026](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-aiuc1-owasp-agentic-crosswalk-20260804-csa/)
- Only 28% of organizations can trace agent actions to a human sponsor (vendor-co-run survey). [CSA Labs, April 12, 2026](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-nist-ai-agent-standards-governance-gap-202)

### Inferences
- **Layer ranking by maturity (analyst judgment):**
  1. **Operator identity / KYA**: deployed (Visa TAP, Mastercard agentic tokens and registration, Amex Agent Registration, Cloudflare signed agents, Entra Agent ID GA). IETF standards are 1–2 years behind but the gap is closing.
  2. **Delegation / scoped authority**: partly deployed. OAuth token exchange and XAA/ID-JAG in enterprise; payment mandates (AP2, Verifiable Intent) in pilots. Cross-domain, multi-hop agent-to-agent delegation is still draft-stage (transaction tokens, identity chaining, AIMS).
  3. **Intent integrity**, meaning assurance that a validly authorized agent's specific commitment reflects the principal's wishes: **unsolved**. Every documented loss sits here.
  4. **Dispute, unwind, and liability**: **unallocated** for non-fraud errors on card rails and absent on crypto rails. Insurance is capped and covers deployer liability, not inter-agent commitments.
- Layers 3 and 4 constrain each other. Because intent integrity cannot be verified, nobody will absorb liability for in-scope errors. Because nobody absorbs liability, there is no economic pressure for an intent-verification standard beyond mandates as dispute evidence. Amex's Agent Purchase Protection is the first attempt to break this loop by having an issuer absorb agent-error risk in exchange for registration plus authenticated intent. It is announced but not yet in effect.
- The practical ceiling on agent commitments today is therefore set by **pre-authorized bounds** (spend caps, merchant allowlists, mandate budgets, closed-loop pilots) and **reversibility** (card rails over crypto rails), not by identity strength. Commitments can grow only as fast as (a) someone underwrites in-bounds errors and (b) mandate evidence becomes decisive in disputes.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data on agent-commitment error rates or dispute volumes in production. Visa reported only "hundreds" of agent transactions as of December 2025, and no 2026 volume figures were found.
- No insurer loss data exists to test whether intent-integrity failures dominate claims.
- Whether EMVCo's reported September 2026 draft framework allocates any liability could not be verified at source.
